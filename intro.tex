% Multinomial, common questions
Multinomial data arise naturally in many applications. Observations are distributed across a finite set of mutually exclusive categories. For example, \citet{zhang2026conjugatingvariationalinferencelarge} used mixed multinomial logit model to analyse the number of consumers choosing among several competing brands in market research. In public health, \citet{Beltran2024} models the number of individuals falling into different health status categories. In text analysis, word counts from a fixed vocabulary are used to characterise sampled documents \citep{Taddy_2015}. These multinomial counts rarely arise in isolation. They are typically associated with covariates describing observations and group structure. The relationship between this structure and the observed counts is of substantive interest. 

A large body of previous work models the category probabilities as a function of covariates and group membership. The multinomial logistic regression model is the classical starting point. It extends naturally to a mixed-effects model that adds a group-level random effect to capture within-group correlation, which is an approach that is still under active development at large scale for multinomial choice data with many groups \citep{zhang2026conjugatingvariationalinferencelarge}. In recent years, there has been a growing movement towards the application of high-dimensional multinomial datasets with large number of categories and groups. 
% Limitations in high-dim cases -> our work (fa-dmr)
However, the methodology of large number of categories is not discussed in the common work, which have arisen in many applications. As mentioned before, vocabulary sizes in text analysis are typically in the thousands \citep{Taddy_2015}, taxon or gene counts in bioinformatics and genomics can run into the thousands \citep{Buettner2015, Tu2023}. In each case, the categories correspond to fine-grained units. Due to the coupled nature of the multinomial distribution, computing the probability of a particular category requires considering the probabilities of all categories. This coupling not only increases the computational cost as the number of categories grows, but also prevents the likelihood from being naturally decomposed, posing challenges for scalable computation and inference. In the meantime, this bottleneck happens when we introduce the group structure across categories for a particular group into multinomial distribution. Its covariance has more free parameters as $Q$ increases. This is far more than any realistic number of groups $J$ can identify. It also means the $Q \times Q$ matrix operations that any likelihood-based procedure needs, such as computing the inverse, cost more regardless of identifiability. Two broad families of estimation strategy are used to fit models of this kind. The first treats the group-level random effects as missing data and integrates them out of the likelihood. This integration is done exactly where feasible, or via a Laplace approximation to the resulting intractable integral within an EM algorithm. The second treats the same integration problem variationally, replacing the true posterior of latent factor with a tractable approximating family, most commonly a mean-field Gaussian fit by coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI). \citet{Goplerud_2024} showed that standard mean-field coordinate ascent variational inference systematically under-estimates posterior uncertainty in high-dimensional mixed models with the degree of under-estimation worsening as dimension grows. \citet{Henderson02102019} faced similar difficulty for the alternative approach of maximum likelihood estimation via the EM algorithm, whose convergence rate is linear. This is a serious practical bottleneck for high-dimensional scenarios.

Multivariate random effects can be represented parsimoniously using a reduced-rank approach, which makes simplifying assumptions that reduce the dimension of the categories to $K\ll Q$ and fit a multivariate random effect via a linear combination of $K$ latent variables. This framework is often referred to as a factor analytical model \citep{Bartholomew2011}, which has been used frequently in ecology and the social sciences. For multinomial distribution, many approaches are also established to simplify the statistical modelling. \citet{Baker1994} exploited the Poisson-Multinomial Equivalence (PME) to decouple the multinomial likelihood as the product of independent-Poisson likelihood conditional on the individual total. \citet{Taddy_2015} adopted this work and established Distributed Multinomial Regression (DMR), which reduced a coupled $Q$-category regression to $Q$ independent Poisson regressions and applied on high-dimensional dataset.  

A key approach for solving this obstacle is the reduced-rank method. Multivariate random effects can be represented parsimoniously using a reduced-rank approach, which makes simplifying assumptions that reduce the effective dimension of the random effect to $K\ll Q$ and fit a multivariate random effect via a linear combination of $K$ latent variables. This framework is often referred to as a factor analytical model \citep{Bartholomew2011}, which has been used frequently in ecology and the social sciences. \citet{Anderson1956StatisticalII} established the statistical inference theory via maximum likelihood for the classical factor analytic model and wrote a covariance matrix as the product of loading matrix plus an idiosyncratic term. Its maximum likelihood estimation is usually carried out via an EM algorithm \citep{Rubin1982}. More recent work develops the asymptotic theory for this class of models. \citet{BAI2012} establishes consistency and asymptotic normality for the factor and loading estimates. This reduced-rank idea has been applied well beyond classical factor analysis. In ecology, generalized linear latent variable models use a low-rank structure to capture correlation among a large number of species \citep{Niku2017}. In plant breeding, multiplicative mixed models impose a factor-analytic structure on genotype-by-environment covariance \citep{Smith2001}. In spatial statistics, a similar low-rank approximation underlies fixed rank kriging \citep{Cressie2008} for large spatial datasets. Closer to the multinomial setting of this thesis, the multivariate Poisson-log normal distribution \citep{Aitchison_1989} was later extended with a low-rank covariance and fitted via variational inference \citep{chiquet2018variational}. \citet{Tu2023} apply the same decomposition directly to logistic-normal-multinomial data for clustering microbiome samples.

There are also many theoretical approaches established to simplify the statistical multinomial modelling. \citet{Baker1994} exploited the Poisson-Multinomail Equivalence (PME) to decouple the multinomial likelihood as the product of independent-Poisson likelihood conditional on the individual total. \citet{Taddy_2015} adopted this work and established Distributed Multinomial Regression (DMR), which reduced a coupled $Q$-category regression to $Q$ independent Poisson regressions and applied on high-dimensional dataset. \citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting} extended this approach from independent to correlated, group-structured multinomial responses with a Gamma-Poisson formulation, and wrote the closed form of marginal likelihood.

In this thesis, we give natural approaches to make inferences between covariates, cluster groups and the multinomial counts. For $i = 1, 2, \dots, I$, $j = 1, 2, \dots, J$ and $q = 1, 2, \dots, Q$, suppose that 

\begin{itemize}
\item  $y_{ijq}$ is the count in category $q$ for the $i$-th observation in cluster group $j$,
\item $\boldsymbol{y}_{ij} = (y_{ij1}, y_{ij2}, \cdots, y_{ijQ})^\top \in \mathbb{Z}^{Q}_{\geq 0}$ is the $Q\times 1$ vector of counts for the $i$-th observation and $j$-th group, 
\item $\bm{v}_{ij}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the $T$-dimensional covariate for the $i$-th observation in the $j$-th group.
\item $\bm{\lambda}_{j}\in \mathbb{R}^{Q}$ is the $Q$-dimensional random effect for the $j$-th group across categories,
\item $\mathbf{f}_{j}\in \mathbb{R}^{K}$ is the $K$-dimensional latent factor for the $j$-th group across categories as missing data.
\end{itemize}

Then we model the factor-analytic and reduced-rank structure discussed above. We assume that the group random effect $\bm{\lambda}$ follws the normal distribution with zero mean, and decompose the covariance as $\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{D}$, then use an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to handle the resulting incomplete-data problem with unobserved $\mathbf{f}$ . Using the Poisson-multinomial equivalence introduced above \citep{Baker1994, Taddy_2015}, we decouple the computation of the Hessian. This accelerates each EM iteration from cubic to near-linear cost in $Q$. We further establish conditions under which the resulting estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal. These conditions are expressed in terms of the joint growth of the number of groups $J$ and the number of categories $Q$, rather than in terms of $Q$ alone. Recent work has pursued a related reduced-rank idea for item-level count data via joint maximum likelihood \citep{Cui2026} instead of the EM algorithm. They discussed EM algorithm and its statistical properties as an open problem since the marginal likelihood maximization suffers more difficulties when dealing with integration of latent factors. In addition, we extend their model structure particularly in allowing genuine idiosyncratic variance to be considered, rather than only the reduced-rank structure.
