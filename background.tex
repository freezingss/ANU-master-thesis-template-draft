\section{Models}
\label{sec:model-setup}
Let $\bm{y}_{ij} = (y_{ij1}, \dots, y_{ijQ})^\top \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^Q$ be the response for $i=1,2,\dots,I_{j}$ observational units in cluster $j=1,2,\dots ,J$. The entries of $\bm{y}_{ij}$ denote the count representing category-specific choices spanning $Q$ distinct categories. A vector of covariates, $\bm{v}_{ij}\in \mathbb{R}^{P}$, may also be recorded for each unit. Let $y_{ij\cdot}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}y_{ijq}$ denote the Multinomial total sum for observation $i$ in group $j(i)$ across $Q$ categories. We omit a constant term from $\bm{v}_{ij}$ to avoid redundancy with the category-specific intercepts already captured by main effects $\bm{\mu}$. Conditional on the total count $y_{ij\cdot}$ and random effects $\bm{\lambda}_{j}\in \mathbb{R}^{Q}$, each observation follows a Multinomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{y}_{ij} \mid  y_{ij\cdot} , \bm{\lambda}_{j}
    \sim \operatorname{Multinomial}\left(y_{ij\cdot},\, \bm{p}_{ij}\right), \quad p_{ijq}=\frac{\exp(\eta_{ijq})}{\sum_{q'=1}^{Q}\exp(\eta_{ijq'})},
    \label{eq:conditional-pm}
\end{equation}
with log-linear predictor,
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{ijq}=\mu_{q}+\bm{v}_{ij}^{\top}\bm{\phi}_{q}+
    \lambda_{jq}, \quad q=1,2,\dots, Q,
    \label{eq:linear-predictor}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{q}\in\mathbb{R}$ is the category-specific intercept. In this thesis, we focus on the contribution $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$ of group $j$ to the log-relative-rate of category $q$. Equation~\eqref{eq:linear-predictor} extends the linear predictor of DMR \citep{Taddy_2015} and of the Poisson extension of \citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting}; the three models differ only in the distribution placed on $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$.

The softmax link in \eqref{eq:conditional-pm} is invariant under the shift $\eta_{ijq}\mapsto\eta_{ijq}+c_{ij}$ for any constant $c_{ij}$ common to all categories, so the intercepts $\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{Q})$ are identified only up to an additive constant. We remove the indeterminacy by sum-to-zero constraint $\sum_{q}\mu_{q}=0$, which automatically restores by the Poisson representation introduced below.

% Need to polish the writing
Throughout, we treat the total sum $y_{ij\cdot}$ as ancillary for $(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Phi},\bm{\lambda}_{j})$. We assume its generating mechanism carries no information about these parameters beyond what they already encoded, so conditioning on $y_{ij\cdot}$ in \eqref{eq:conditional-pm} loses no relevant information. This is standard in Multinomial regression, but it is not automatic. We do not attempt a joint model of volumn and composition  here. To be clear, ancillarity is about information, not determinism: the total is still random, its distribution just carries no extra information about $(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Phi},\bm{\lambda}_{j})$. In Section~\ref{sec:PME}, we use the Poisson-Multinomial equivalence to represent each $y_{ijq}$ as an independent Poisson variate, whose offset $\xi_{ij}$ absorbs the (random) total. Conditioning on $y_{ij\cdot}$ recovers the Multinomial conditional in \eqref{eq:conditional-pm} exactly. We treat $\xi_{ij}$ as a computational device but not a modeling claim about the total.

The group random effects are assumed independently and identically distributed:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{\lambda}_{j}\sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}), \quad \bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{BB}^{\top}+\mathbf{D},
    \label{eq: Overall notation structure}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{B}\in \mathbb{R}^{Q\times K}$ is the factor loadings matrix and $\mathbf{D}$ is the variance. Equivalently, in the factor regression form:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{\lambda}_{j}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}_{j}+
    \bm{\epsilon}_{j}, \quad \mathbf{f}_{j}\sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{K}), \quad \bm{\epsilon}_{j}\sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{D}),
    \label{eq: Detailed notation structure}
\end{equation}
so that $\lambda_{jq}=\mathbf{b}_{q}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{j}+\epsilon_{jq}$, where $\mathbf{b}_{q}$ is the q-th row of $\mathbf{B}$. We assume each group $j$ has a $K$-dimensional factor $\bm{f}_{j}$ choosing from $Q$ distinct categories, where $K$ is known. In this application, each loading entries of $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$ represents the cluster information by measuring the significance of how cluster $j$ loads on each of the latent factors. We adopt the category of orthogonal latent factors \citep{Cui2026}, which assumed to be independent and illustrated the relationship of factors in our group random effects \eqref{eq: Detailed notation structure}. Finally, we assume $\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{Q}$ with $\sigma^{2} >0$ rather than the fully diagonal $\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(d_{1}^{2},\dots,d_{Q}^{2})$ with entries $d_{q}>0$ for the model simplification.

It also keeps the parameter count in $\mathbf{D}$ from growing with $Q$. As a further benefit, this choice simplifies the Woodbury reduction introduced in Chapter~\ref{cha:computational-acceleration}, whose complexity expression is considerably harder to state under the fully diagonal assumption.

This approach makes it possible to fit large multivariate random effects, because the number of parameters required in the variance-covariance matrix is now reduced to linearity with $Q$.

The factor structure \eqref{eq: Detailed notation structure} is subject to classical indeterminacy, which includes both rotational and sign indeterminacy. For any $K\times K$ orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{V}$, the substitution $(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{f}_{j})\to (\mathbf{BV},\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{j})$ leaves the distribution of $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$ unchanged. Also, the substitution $(\mathbf{b}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{j})\to (-\mathbf{b}_{k},-\mathbf{f}_{j})$ product of $\mathbf{b}_{q}\mathbf{f}_{k}$ is the same as $(-\mathbf{b}_{q})(-\mathbf{f}_{k})$.

    \begin{equation*}
        \mathbf{B}_{[1:2,]}=
        \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & a_{12} & a_{13} \\
            a_{21} & 0 & 0 
        \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{G}_{\theta}=
        \begin{pmatrix}

            0 & -\sin \theta & \cos \theta
        \end{pmatrix}.
    \end{equation*}
    For any $\theta$, 
    \begin{equation*}
        [\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}_{\theta}]_{[1:2,]}
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & *_{12} & *_{13} \\
            a_{21} & 0 & 0 
        \end{pmatrix}.
    \end{equation*}
    
    For any $\theta$, the zero pattern of $[\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G}_{\theta}]_{[1:2,]}$ retains the same as $\mathbf{B}_{[1:2,]}$. Thus, the above transformation matrix $\mathbf{G}_{\theta}$ leaves the factor covariance matrix unchanged, which is not rotationally identifiable under this constraint. 
    \label{eg:no-rotational}
\end{example}

Example~\eqref{eg:no-rotational} shows that a necessary condition for identifiability is that no two columns of the loading matrix share the same zero pattern. To avoid these cases, we consider the following sufficient condition that covers many commonly used identifiability constraints. Thus, we give the positive lower triangular constraint to recover the true value of matrix $\mathbf{B}$, 

\begin{equation}
    b_{qk}=0 \text{ for } q<k,\quad b_{kk}>0 \text{ for }k=1,2,\dots ,K,
    \label{eq: PLT}
\end{equation}
which solves the rotationally indeterminacy and sign indeterminacy. \citet{Cui2026} had proved many sufficient constraints, and we adapt their IC$^{(2)}$ with orthogonal latent variables in factor analysis.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

Multinomial settings share a common structure with $I$ observations in $J$ groups, whose individual observation is a count vector $\bm{y}_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}^Q_{\geq 0}$ over $Q$ categories. However, the inference of original Multinomial models is challenging due to the log-linear softmax link and the group random effects. The softmax link is coupling while it requires updating through all $Q$ categories, in the meanwhile, the hierarchical aspect ignoring group structure inflates Type~I error and introduces confounding bias in fixed-effect estimates \citep{Lee2019AnalysisOG}. The literature has addressed these two dimensions largely in isolation. 

The simplest approach treats observations as exchangeable and attributes all variation to measured covariates by Multinomial logistic regression. However, the maximum likelihood is computationally prohibitive owing to the coupled softmax normalizer with large number of categories. \citet{10.2307/2348134} established the Poisson--Multinomial Equivalence (PME). That is, conditional on the total count, the Multinomial likelihood (up to a constant) can be surrogate by the product of $Q$ independent Poisson log-linear models with a common additive offset. \citet{Taddy_2015} exploited this to propose Distributed Multinomial Regression (DMR) and applied it to large-vocabulary document classification. \citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting} subsequently formalized and extended the PME framework clarifying conditions under which it applies to a broad class of Multinomial regression models.
 
Standard Multinomial logistic regression and distributed Multinomial regression remain important baselines, but their central limitation is the absence of group-level random effects. When groups differ systematically, treating observations as exchangeable absorbs unmodeled group variation into fixed-effect estimates. For the high-dimensional fixed effects, \citet{Correia_2020} provides efficient Poisson estimation algorithms. Our work builds on this computational tradition while additionally modeling group-level structure.

With the improvement of science and technology, various fields, for example, psychology, economics and genetics, require statistical tool to analyze large-scale data, not only with huge sample size, but also with more variates (categories). Poisson Lognormal (PLN) was introduced by \citet{Aitchison_1989}, placing a Gaussian prior $\bm{Z}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}, \bm{\Sigma})$ on the log-linear predictors and drawing $Y_{ijq} \mid Z_{ijq} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\bigl(\exp(Z_{iq})\bigr)$ independently. PLN captures between-category correlations and over-dispersion. However, they can not estimate the covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$ directly when $Q$ is larger than expected. \citet{chiquet2018variational} addressed this using PLN-PCA, which constraining $\bm{\Sigma} = \mathbf{CC}^\top$ with $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times K}$ and $K\ll Q$, then used variational EM with a mean-field Gaussian approximation. The key limitations are that PLN-PCA operates at the observation level only, with no group random effect and idiosyncratic covariance matrix. \citet{Tu2023} introduced logistic-normal multinomial (LNM) factor analysis with a factor-structured observation-level covariance $\bm{\Sigma} = \mathbf{BB}^{\top} + \bm{\Psi}$, $\bm{\Psi} = \mathrm{diag}(\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_Q)$. The EM algorithm uses a variational Gaussian approximation in the E-step and the Rubin-Thayer iterations \citep{Rubin1982} in the M-step. Their LNM-FA shares the factor-analytic motivation of the present work, which motivated our work targeting to structure a group-level random effect $\bm{\lambda}_j = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}_j + \bm{\epsilon}_j$ and adopting the spherical simplification $\mathbf{D} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_Q$. In parallel, \citet{mcgillycuddy2024parsimoniouslyfittinglargemultivariate} implemented a reduced-rank parameterization of large multivariate random effects within the general glmmTMB framework. Their work showed that factor-structured random effects are practically useful for grouped count data, not just a theoretical construct. However, their approach still operates at the observation level. It lacks the group-level hierarchical structure that is central to our work.

\citet{Cui2026} studied a purely bi-linear factor structure without idiosyncratic-noise. They treated the factors $\mathbf{f}$ as fixed parameters and estimating them by joint likelihood method. Their asymptotic theory establishes a scaling condition under which factor-loading estimates are asymptotically normal, requiring both dimensions to diverge jointly. They discussed about the drawbacks of marginal maximum likelihood estimation, including the prior distribution for latent variables, integrals computational challenge and critical theoretical analysis of the marginal estimator. Motivated by their work, we define an exact example of the grouped Multinomial data, then use the marginal maximum likelihood estimation under the framework of PME as the extension of their work.

\section{Poisson-Multinomial Equivalence}
\label{sec:PME}
    
The Poisson-Multinomial Equivalence established by \citet{10.2307/2348134} states that the Multinomial likelihood conditional on $y_{ij\cdot}$ is exactly equivalent to a product of independent Poisson likelihoods sharing a common additive offset,

\begin{equation}
    \xi_{ij} = \log y_{ij\cdot} - \log\sum_{q=1}^Q \exp(\eta_{ijq}),
    \label{eq:xi_baker}
\end{equation}

The joint Multinomial draw $\bm{y}_{ij} \mid \bm{\lambda}_{j} \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}(y_{ij\cdot}, \bm{p}_{ij})$ is equivalent to $y_{ijq} \mid \bm{\lambda}_{j} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\exp(\eta_{ijq} + \xi_{ij}))$ independently for $q = 1, \ldots, Q$, conditional on $y_{ij\cdot}$. We give the definition of $\eta_{ijq}$ and $\bm{p}_{ij}$ in section~\ref{sec:two-level-hierarchy}. The offset~\eqref{eq:xi_baker} absorbs the softmax normalizer and decouples the $Q$ Poisson problems, thus we can solve these $Q$ Poisson independently. 

\citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting} extended the framework of \citet{10.2307/2348134} by treating the aggregate count $y_{ij\cdot}$ as Poisson-distributed given the group effect $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$, rather than fixing it as the conditioning variable in the Multinomial likelihood. Marginalizing over this random aggregate recovers the same factorization into independent Poisson components across categories $q = 1, \dots, Q$. As a result, $\xi_{ij}$ becomes the rate parameter of this induced Poisson distribution instead of a fixed conditioning quantity.

This chapter carries the same idea one step further into E-step. In the standard Laplace E-step, $\bm{\lambda}_j$ is updated via Newton's method~\eqref{eq:newton-method} on the full Multinomial log-posterior $\ell(\bm{\lambda}_j)$. The bottleneck is the term in Hessian $\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_j} y_{ij\cdot} \bm{p}_{ij} \bm{p}_{ij}^\top$, which comes from the softmax normalizer with rank-$I_j$. This term couples all $Q$ categories together, so each Newton step requires an $O(Q^3)$ linear solve. Inspired by the insight of~\citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting}, we introduce $\xi_{ij}$ as an explicit auxiliary variable in the E-step. Holding $\xi_{ij}$ fixed while updating $\bm{\lambda}_j$ removes the log-sum-exp coupling entirely, so the Hessian reduces to a diagonal matrix, enabling a second Woodbury reduction cutting the per-step cost from $O(Q(I_j + K))$ down to $O(QK^2 + K^3)$.

\section{EM Algorithm \& Relatives}
\label{sec: em-algorithm-relatives}

In order to have a good understanding for further discussion, we briefly review the classical EM algorithm in this section. Then we discuss the models that have the similar structure with our model. 

Let $Y$ and $Z$ be random variables taking values in the sample spaces $\mathcal{Y}$ and $\mathcal{Z}$, respectively. Suppose that the pair $(Y,Z)$ has a joint density function $f_{\theta^{*}}$ that belongs to some parameterized family $\{f_{\theta}\mid \theta\in \Omega
\}$, for a non-empty compact convex set $\Omega$. Rather than observing complete data $(Y,Z)$, we observe only component $Y$.Thus, the component $Z$ corresponds to the missing or latent structure in the data. Our goal is to obtain an estimate of the unknown parameter $\theta^{*}$ via maximum likelihood to compute some $\hat{\theta}\in\Omega$ maximizing the function $\theta\mapsto g_{\theta}(y)$, where

\begin{equation}
    g_{\theta}(y)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}f_{\theta}(y,z)\,dz
    \label{eq:classical-em-integral}
\end{equation}
is the density function of the observed variable $Y$.

In many settings, computing log likelihood $\log f_{\theta}(y,z)$ of both latent variables and observed data is easier and computationally cheaper than evaluating the log likelihood of the observed data itself. The EM algorithm is well-suited to such settings. For each $\theta\in\Omega$, let $k_{\theta}(z\mid y)$ denote the conditional density of $z$ given by $y$, then the log likelihood at $\theta^{'}\in\Omega$ can be lower bounded using Jensen's inequality
\begin{equation}
    \log g_{\theta'}(y) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{Z}} k_\theta(z \mid y) \log f_{\theta'}(y,z)\, dz}_{Q(\theta' \mid \theta)} \;-\; \int_{\mathcal{Z}} k_\theta(z \mid y) \log k_\theta(z \mid y)\, dz,
    \label{eq:classical-em-jensen}
\end{equation}
the equality holds when $\theta=\theta^{'}$. Thus, we have a family of lower bounds on the log likelihood, denoted by $Q(\cdot\mid \cdot)$. We maximize this lower bound in M-step
\begin{equation}
    \theta^{(t+1)}=\arg\max_{\theta^{'}\in\Omega}Q(\theta^{'}\mid \theta^{(t)}),
    \label{eq:em-m-step}
\end{equation}
and then re-evaluates the lower bound at the new parameter value in E-step.
